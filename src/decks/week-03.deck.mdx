# Comparative warding

Week 3 — nine traditions, named as themselves

---

## Named, not renamed

This course does not file real practice under a dry in-universe label.
The point is that you could plausibly recognize these from your own
life. A renamed version would only hide that.

---

## Five core traditions

1. **Iron and salt** at doorway thresholds
2. **Fú (符) talisman** consecration
3. **Spirit walls** and the "mouth of Qi"
4. **Mezuzah** placement
5. **Ofuda, kamidana, shimenawa**

Studied as full procedural case studies, broken into procedure, sequence,
and a documented limit or failure for each.

9. **Mirror-covering during shiva** — a household's most vulnerable week

# How to use these in practice

Procedure and sequence only matter if you can apply them to a case. What
follows is a countermeasure reference, not folklore trivia.

---

## Reading a measure as a countermeasure claim

Every tradition on this week's list makes an implicit, testable claim.
Read it the same way each time:

- **when** is it used — ongoing protection, or a one-off response to a
  diagnosed problem?
- **how** is it performed or placed — the exact procedure the tradition
  specifies, beyond just naming the object?
- **is there a sequence** — stages that have to happen in order?
- **what's its limit** — does it wear out, need renewal, or only guard
  one point?
- **what does documented failure look like**, if any is recorded?

---

## Worked example: the iron/salt pairing

**When:** ongoing, passive protection of doors, windows, hearths.
**How:** iron nailed into frames and hearthstones; salt sprinkled across
the same thresholds.
**Sequence:** none — a single installation, treated as permanent.
**Limit:** salt dissolves in rain and gets tracked away; iron doesn't.
**Failure:** sources are explicit that salt "washes away," which is
*why* it's never used alone.

## Worked example: the fú talisman

**When:** commissioned case-by-case, for a specific problem.
**How:** cinnabar ink on yellow paper, one continuous brushstroke.
**Sequence:** four fixed stages — purify and invoke, draw, seal,
consecrate.
**Limit:** none once complete.
**Failure:** skipping any of the four stages reportedly yields "an
approximation," not a functioning fú: a countermeasure applied
incompletely isn't a weaker version, it's a different, non-working thing.

---

## Worked example: the English witch bottle

**When:** one-off, following a diagnosed affliction.
**How:** pins, nails, and the afflicted person's hair sealed inside an
ordinary bottle, then buried or heated.
**Sequence:** two-stage — diagnose first, construct and activate second.
**Limit:** must be heated to the point of shattering to fully activate.
**Failure:** if the cork pops before the bottle shatters, containment is
incomplete, and the harm is believed to escape anyway.

---

## What this means for your field case files

Weeks 5–6 will ask you to state not just *that* a countermeasure applies,
but *how precisely* it was executed, matching the same when / how /
sequence / limit reasoning modelled here. A countermeasure that skips a
stage or misses a renewal isn't a smaller version of the real thing.

## Where the nine converge

No documented contact between these traditions, and yet the emphasis
falls again and again on **transitions** (doorways, corners, mirrors)
and on **precision of execution** as the line between a working
countermeasure and an inert prop.
