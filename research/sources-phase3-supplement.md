# Research sources — Phase 3 supplement (SCP-093 replacement + additions)

**Scope note.** Requested 2026-09-21, as a follow-up research pass after
`course-logic-proposal.md` §0 demoted SCP-093 ("Red Sea Object") from the
weeks 7–8 desk-study corpus for failing the ordinary-life screening test
(an archaeological relic is exotic-object horror, not something an
ordinary student could plausibly encounter). This file searches for a
strong replacement plus supplementary candidates, screened against the
same test: *is this grounded in something an ordinary person could
plausibly brush up against — a photograph, an arcade game, a chain email,
a children's game, a mirror, a piece of candy?* Not exotic occultism,
alien artifacts, or secret-facility mythology.

This file does **not** touch `sources.md` or any other existing research
file — per instructions, this is a standalone addition, written once. It
does not duplicate any of the 9 items already kept in `sources.md`'s
"Phase 3 corpus — kept candidates" section (SCP-173, SCP-3008, SCP-093,
SCP-2317, Smile Dog/smile.jpg, Kuchisake-onna, Backrooms Level 0, Ted the
Caver, LOCAL58).

**11 candidates screened, 8 kept, 3 rejected outright** (a fourth —
Ben Drowned — is kept but flagged for an overlap risk, see below). All
kept items are classified against the department's real four-category
scheme (persistent / recurring / responsive / dormant, non-exclusive)
where the item is a case; two items (Polybius, the Halloween-candy
legend) are themselves meta-cases about corroboration/legend-transmission
rather than anomaly entities, and are classified as such rather than
forced into the four categories.

**Overlap-risk note (Ben Drowned):** `research/sources-phase1-supplement.md`
did not exist at the time of this pass (checked via directory listing).
If a parallel Phase 1 research pass later adds Ben Drowned independently,
dedupe against this entry rather than keeping both.

## Kept candidates

### Week 7 — regional-feeling corpus (adds to the existing 5)

#### 1. Polybius (arcade-game urban legend)
- **URL:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polybius_(urban_legend) ; earliest
  print reference GamePro, September 2003; earliest online reference
  coinop.org, 1998, with the legend crystallizing on a February 2000
  CoinOp.org listing
- **Author/site:** decentralized legend, no single author; Wikipedia
  article is the most complete debunking/history summary
- **Language:** English · **Classification:** meta-case — a legend *about*
  a case, with zero physical evidence of the case itself ever existing
- Premise: a mysterious arcade cabinet supposedly appeared in Portland,
  Oregon arcades in 1981, hooking players into obsessive replay before
  causing amnesia, seizures, and night terrors, after which unmarked
  government agents removed every unit and erased the record. Rule/
  mechanic: none stated for the game itself — the "mechanic" that matters
  for this course is the legend's own structure, since no cabinet, company
  registration, or contemporary account of it has ever surfaced, and the
  name is very plausibly a deliberate literary joke (the real Polybius was
  an ancient historian who insisted on verifiable evidence). Why it passes
  the ordinary-life test: an arcade cabinet is about as ordinary a piece of
  early-1980s consumer technology as exists — no occultism, no artifact,
  just a machine a kid could have played. What it teaches: a clean, fully
  documented case of a legend with *zero* surviving physical evidence,
  useful paired against SCP-2317 (over-solid official sourcing) and Smile
  Dog (structurally uncorroborable) as a third distinct failure mode —
  "no evidence ever existed in the first place," rather than "compromised
  witnesses" or "unverified procedure." SlopU citation sketch: "an
  arcade-linked case retained despite the total absence of a surviving
  specimen, catalogued and register-checked with no corroborating
  installation record found in any facility's equipment logs — read for
  its total-absence structure, not weighted in the convergence analysis."
- **Week fit:** 7.

#### 2. Halloween candy tampering ("Halloween sadism")
- **URL:** overview at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisoned_candy_myths ;
  primary scholarship: Joel Best and Gerald T. Horiuchi, "The Razor Blade
  in the Apple: The Social Construction of Urban Legends," *Social
  Problems* 32:5 (1985), 488–499; updated in Joel Best, *Threatened
  Children: Rhetoric and Concern about Child-Victims*, University of
  Chicago Press, 1990; ongoing commentary at https://www.joelbest.net/halloween-sadism
- **Author/site:** Joel Best, professor of sociology and criminal justice,
  University of Delaware — the recognized authority on this specific
  legend, still giving comment on it decades later
- **Language:** English · **Classification:** N/A — a real sociological
  case study of legend transmission and moral panic, not an anomaly entity
- Premise: the recurring claim that strangers hide razor blades, needles,
  or poison in Halloween candy handed to trick-or-treaters. Best and
  Horiuchi's newspaper survey (four papers, 1958–84) found 76 reported
  tampering incidents, injuries in only 20, and effectively no verified
  stranger-poisoning deaths — the one real fatal case (excluded here per
  the exclusion rule against real named individuals) was later shown to be
  a family member using the holiday as cover, not a stranger. Best frames
  this explicitly as a *legend* (not merely a false belief) that peaked
  alongside real, unrelated events (the Tylenol tampering scare) and
  persists because it gives shape to broader anxiety about strangers and
  children. Why it passes the ordinary-life test: trick-or-treating candy
  is about as ordinary and domestic as source material gets — no
  occultism, just a paper bag of sweets. What it teaches: this is the
  strongest available bridge between the course's own Brunvand citation
  (already verified real in `01-theory-audit.md`) and Dégh/Vázsonyi's
  ostension typology (already added in `sources.md`'s Phase 4 section) —
  a real, academically tracked case of a legend prompting real defensive
  behaviour (candy X-raying, hospital "candy check" events) despite
  near-zero verified incidents, i.e. legend-as-fact in exactly the
  ostension sense the course already cites. SlopU citation sketch: "a
  long-running seasonal advisory pattern, cross-referenced against actual
  incident logs over several decades and found to correlate poorly with
  reported concern — retained as the department's standard illustration of
  why perceived case volume and corroborated case volume are tracked as
  two separate figures."
- **Week fit:** 7.

#### 3. Petscop (2017–2019 web series)
- **URL:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petscop ; channel and episode
  index at https://petscop.fandom.com/wiki/Petscop
- **Author/site:** Tony Domenico (sole creator, publicly revealed
  November 2019; anonymous throughout the 2017–2019 run)
- **Language:** English · **Classification:** responsive (the "game"
  reveals content contingent on the player's specific in-game actions),
  recurring (new videos re-trigger fresh unease with each release)
- Premise: a YouTube channel presents itself as a Let's Play of "Petscop,"
  a supposedly unreleased 1990s PlayStation game the uploader (a
  pseudonymous player, "Paul") claims to have obtained secondhand. What
  begins as an ordinary, slightly awkward pet-collecting game gradually
  reveals hidden rooms, corrupted save states, and disturbing implications
  about the game's real subject matter. Rule/mechanic: no stated
  supernatural rule — the horror is structural, built entirely from
  plausible-looking but off menu options, glitch-like anomalies, and a
  found-footage frame (a real person claiming to have found a real,
  physical secondhand disc). Why it passes the ordinary-life test: a
  battered secondhand game disc bought or found used is exactly the kind
  of ordinary consumer object the design principle asks for — no exotic
  artifact, just old media. What it teaches: a format lesson distinct from
  Ted the Caver's real-time diary — here the "evidence" is video footage
  of software behaviour, useful for extending week 7's sourcing lesson to
  a case where the reviewer has to assess whether *in-game* behaviour is
  even possible on real period hardware, a distinct verification skill
  from checking a witness's claims. SlopU citation sketch: "a secondhand-
  media case retained for its serialised disclosure structure; unusually,
  the corpus tracks the reporter's software claims against known hardware
  behaviour as an independent verification channel."
- **Week fit:** 7.

#### 4. BEN Drowned / "Haunted Majora's Mask Cartridge" — *overlap-risk flagged*
- **URL:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Drowned ;
  https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/majoras-mask-creepypasta-ben-drowned
- **Author/site:** Alex Hall ("Jadusable"); first posted to 4chan's /x/,
  September 7, 2010, revealed as a college creative-writing project on
  September 20, 2010, then continued as an alternate-reality game through 2020
- **Language:** English · **Classification:** responsive (in-game
  behaviour reacts to specific player actions, e.g. deleting a save file),
  recurring
- Premise: a poster describes buying a secondhand Nintendo 64 and a
  used copy of *The Legend of Zelda: Majora's Mask* at a yard sale from an
  elderly stranger, finding an existing save file named "BEN," and, after
  deleting it, finding non-player characters begin addressing him by that
  name and the game glitching (reversed music, vanished textures). Rule/
  mechanic: none formally stated, but a consistent escalation tied
  directly to specific player choices (deleting the file, continuing to
  play) — a "you keep playing, it keeps escalating" structure. Why it
  passes the ordinary-life test: a used game cartridge bought at a yard
  sale is an archetypal ordinary secondhand object — the entire premise
  depends on it being unremarkable enough to buy without a second thought.
  What it teaches: the creator's own public confirmation that it began as
  a deliberate creative-writing exercise, yet continued to expand as an
  audience-participation ARG for a decade afterward, makes this the
  strongest available case for teaching the difference between a legend's
  *origin* (openly authored, not concealed) and its *life* (continuing
  circulation and elaboration regardless) — a distinct lesson from Smile
  Dog's anonymous origin. **Flag:** check for duplication against
  `research/sources-phase1-supplement.md` if/when it exists — the prompt
  that generated this file noted a parallel Phase 1 pass might cover this
  same case; if so, keep whichever write-up is more developed and drop
  the other rather than running both.
- **Week fit:** 7.

#### 5. SCP-1048 — "Builder Bear"
- **URL:** https://scp-wiki.wikidot.com/scp-1048 · SCP Foundation Wiki
  (EN), object class Euclid
- **Author/site:** SCP Foundation collaborative wiki
- **Language:** English · **Classification:** persistent (ongoing,
  affectionate presence under observation), with a non-exclusive dormant
  secondary trait (violent construction behaviour occurs only unobserved)
- Premise: an ordinary-looking 33cm teddy bear that moves under its own
  power, communicates through simple gestures, and behaves with
  consistent, genuine-seeming affection toward everyone it interacts with
  — hugging legs, dancing, drawing pictures. Only months after being
  secured does staff discover it periodically constructs crude replica
  "bears" from whatever scavenged materials are on hand (metal scraps,
  worse), each exhibiting sudden, extreme violence toward people, in stark
  contrast to the original's demeanour. Rule/mechanic: the affection is
  hypothesized to be functional — a means of maintaining trust and gaining
  unsupervised access to materials, not evidence of benign intent. Why it
  passes the ordinary-life test: a stuffed toy is the most domestic object
  imaginable — every part of the horror is built from an object children
  are handed, not an artifact or a facility. What it teaches: a clean
  teaching case for *persistent-plus-dormant* co-classification (the
  entity's baseline behaviour and its rare, dangerous behaviour don't
  contradict each other, they're two tracked channels of the same case) —
  useful contrast to SCP-173's purely responsive profile. SlopU citation
  sketch: "a companion-object case, notable for a consistently observed
  benign baseline that several independent handlers describe in identical
  terms, alongside a separate, rarely observed and unrelated hazardous
  behaviour recorded only in its absence from direct supervision."
- **Week fit:** 7.

### Week 8 — global/cross-cultural corpus (adds to the existing 4)

#### 6. Bloody Mary (mirror-summoning ritual)
- **URL:** foundational scholarship: Janet L. Langlois, "'Mary Whales, I
  Believe in You': Myth and Ritual Subdued," *Indiana Folklore* 11 (1978);
  Alan Dundes's response essay, "Bloody Mary in the Mirror: A Ritual
  Reflection of Pre-Pubescent Anxiety" (widely mirrored, e.g.
  https://home.iscte-iul.pt/~fgvs/Dundes%20bloody.pdf ); overview at
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloody_Mary_(folklore)
- **Author/site:** Janet L. Langlois (folklorist, then Wayne State
  University); Alan Dundes (folklorist, UC Berkeley) engaging directly
  with her work; both real, peer-reviewed folklore scholarship
- **Language:** English · **Classification:** responsive (behaviour is
  entirely gated on performing the ritual correctly), recurring
  (re-triggerable indefinitely, by anyone, anywhere there's a mirror)
- Premise: a participant, usually in a darkened bathroom, says a name
  (commonly "Bloody Mary," though Langlois's original 1970s fieldwork
  recorded the variant name "Mary Whales") a fixed number of times while
  looking into a mirror, summoning a spectral face — sometimes benign,
  sometimes violently dangerous depending on the local variant. Rule/
  mechanic: name repetition count, darkness, and a mirror are the three
  consistently required elements across documented variants; Langlois's
  key scholarly finding, later endorsed by Dundes, is that what actually
  appears in participants' accounts is frequently their own reflection,
  reframed. Why it passes the ordinary-life test: this is the purest
  possible instance of the design principle — an ordinary household
  bathroom mirror, no object, no location beyond a room every student has
  stood in. What it teaches: real, decades-deep peer-reviewed folklore
  scholarship (not just a repeated internet claim) analyzing a
  ritual-game as *pre-adolescent identity anxiety projected onto a
  reflective surface* — directly extends the course's existing Freud
  citation (the uncanny as the return of the familiar) with a documented
  real-world case where scholars made exactly that argument about a real,
  ongoing children's practice. SlopU citation sketch: "a mirror-ritual
  case, notable for a strong scholarly consensus that a majority of
  reported sightings describe the summoner's own reflection under altered
  lighting — retained as the corpus's clearest instance of an anomaly
  report converging on a documented perceptual rather than external cause."
- **Week fit:** 8 (pairs directly with item 7 below).

#### 7. Hanako-san (花子さん) — the third-stall school ghost
- **URL:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanako-san ; comparative folklore
  discussed in Diane E. Goldstein, Sylvia Ann Grider & Jeannie Banks
  Thomas, *Haunting Experiences: Ghosts in Contemporary Folklore*, Utah
  State University Press, 2007; earliest written trace: Miyoko Matsutani,
  *Gendai Minwakō* 7 (1948, recording an Iwate Prefecture variant)
- **Author/site:** decentralized oral tradition, first written record by
  folklorist Miyoko Matsutani; modern academic treatment via Goldstein/
  Grider/Thomas
- **Language:** Japanese-origin, English secondary sources ·
  **Classification:** responsive (appearance/reply gated on the exact
  knock-and-ask sequence), recurring
- Premise: a schoolgirl ghost said to haunt a specific stall (commonly
  the third) of a school bathroom, most often on the third floor. A
  student knocks three times, asks "Hanako-san, are you there?", and may
  or may not receive a reply — accounts of what happens next vary widely
  by region and telling. Rule/mechanic: the ritual's specific counted
  elements (which floor, which stall, how many knocks) are the
  consistently transmitted part, while Hanako's backstory (an air-raid
  death, bullying, murder) varies freely by teller — folklorists note this
  is backwards from most ghost lore, where the *method* is what's shared
  reliably and the *biography* is the unstable part. Why it passes the
  ordinary-life test: a school bathroom stall is exactly the kind of
  ordinary institutional space the design principle asks for, and the
  legend is explicitly tied to the shared architecture of school life
  rather than to any historical grievance site. What it teaches: paired
  directly with Bloody Mary, this gives week 8 a textbook instance of its
  own stated spec — two ritual-summoning legends with the same
  responsive-ghost-in-an-institutional-fixture structure (knock-and-ask
  vs. name-and-look), independently documented in two countries with no
  plausible line of transmission between them, converging on the same
  classification-relevant shape. SlopU citation sketch: "an East Asian
  institutional-space case, included for its knock-count/question
  structure — a scripted-encounter mechanism directly comparable to a
  Western mirror-ritual case already in the corpus, despite no
  documented contact between the two traditions."
- **Week fit:** 8 (pairs directly with item 6 above).

#### 8. The Charlie Charlie Challenge (pencil-balancing divination game)
- **URL:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Charlie_challenge ;
  contemporary BBC Mundo reporting on the pencil-game ("Juego de la
  Lapicera") lineage, summarized at
  https://theghostinmymachine.com/2020/09/16/the-most-dangerous-games-charlie-charlie-challenge-pencils-el-juego-de-lapices-lapicera-charly-martha-martita/
- **Author/site:** decentralized regional tradition (juegos de lápices,
  a documented family of Spanish-language pencil-and-paper divination
  games) plus a 2015 English-language internet viral variant; no single
  author
- **Language:** originates in Spanish-language folk practice, English
  secondary sources for the 2015 viral wave · **Classification:**
  responsive (the pencil's movement is treated as an answer to a posed
  question), recurring
- Premise: two pencils are balanced in a cross shape over a sheet of
  paper marked "yes"/"no"; players ask "Charlie, Charlie, are you there?"
  and treat the top pencil's subsequent drift as the spirit's answer.
  Rule/mechanic: the balancing setup itself is the entire mechanism — no
  object beyond paper and two pencils, and the "yes/no" branching is
  literally built into the paper's layout. Why it passes the ordinary-life
  test: pencils and a sheet of paper are about the most ordinary
  schoolroom objects available — this is genuinely a game any student
  could set up at their own desk in under a minute. Note on sourcing
  integrity (worth teaching explicitly): although widely reported as an
  authentic "Mexican demon game," folklore-adjacent journalists (e.g. BBC
  Mundo's Maria Elena Navez) found no actual Mexican demon by that name,
  and the 2015 "Charlie" branding and viral spread appear to be an
  English-internet overlay on a real, older, and broader Spanish-language
  pencil-game family (juegos de lápices) — a distinct case from real
  cultural origin. What it teaches: a live example of exactly the
  corroboration-quality lesson week 7 opens with, but applied
  cross-culturally — a real regional tradition (the pencil-game family)
  getting a fabricated specific brand-name and an invented single-country
  origin story layered on top of it during viral spread, which the
  department's own sourcing method would have to catch. SlopU citation
  sketch: "a paper-and-pencil divination case, included despite a
  documented mismatch between its popularly claimed single-country origin
  and the broader, older regional practice family it was actually drawn
  from — retained as a worked example of separating a practice's real
  lineage from its viral branding."
- **Week fit:** 8 — works either as a third leg alongside items 6–7
  (three ritual-game traditions, three regions, one shared mechanism
  family: name/knock/balance-and-ask) or standalone if the week 8 slide
  deck only wants a pair.

## Rejected candidates (screened, not kept)

- **Momo Challenge (2018–19 WhatsApp hoax).** Real, well-documented hoax
  (the viral face is a repurposed 2016 sculpture by Japanese artist
  Keisuke Aiso; investigators in India and elsewhere found no verified
  harm). Rejected on tonal grounds, not factual grounds: the hoax's actual
  content is a purported instruction chain culminating in child self-harm/
  suicide, which sits too close to real teen-safety moral-panic territory
  (structurally similar to the Blue Whale Challenge, which does have
  contested real-death claims attached) for this course's bureaucratic-
  mundane-eerie register. The Halloween-candy legend (kept, item 2 above)
  covers the same "moral panic sociology" teaching job without that
  specific self-harm framing.
- **Kashima Reiko.** A second, very similar Japanese school-bathroom ghost
  (asks victims where her legs are, said to have been cut in half by a
  train). Rejected for redundancy with Hanako-san (item 7) — same slot,
  same mechanism family, no independent teaching point beyond what
  Hanako-san already covers; noting it here in case a future pass wants a
  *third* bathroom-ghost example specifically.
- **Slender Man.** Considered seriously, since Tolbert's "reverse
  ostension" analysis of Slender Man is already cited as real scholarship
  in `sources.md`'s Phase 4 section — using the case itself here would
  close that loop. Rejected anyway: the entity (a preternaturally tall,
  faceless, tentacled figure) is a fabricated monster-of-the-week creature
  once you get past its origin in ordinary manipulated photographs, which
  is a worse ordinary-life fit than Smile Dog (an image with no monster
  attached at all, already kept). Recommend citing the Tolbert scholarship
  on its own merits (already done) without importing Slender Man itself as
  a case.
- **Robert the Doll.** A real, named antique doll on public display at a
  specific real museum (Fort East Martello Museum, Key West), with a
  real attributed original owner. Rejected outright per the exclusion
  rule — identifiable real institution and (contested but real-claimed)
  named individual, regardless of how generic the "cursed doll" motif
  itself is.
- **"The Bye Bye Man."** A name-taboo horror legend (speaking or writing
  the name summons death) compiled by author Robert Damon Schneck from
  family-told stories. Rejected: leans mythic/name-taboo rather than
  ordinary-object, and its actual textual history is closer to a single
  author's compiled personal-family horror anecdote than a broadly
  attested folk tradition — thinner sourcing than any kept item above.

## Sources I could not access or verify

None of the sources cited above were paywalled or blocked. All were
reachable via search-tool summaries of Wikipedia, the SCP Foundation
wiki, Know Your Meme, and the specific academic/journalistic secondary
sources cited per item (Langlois/Dundes on Bloody Mary; Goldstein/Grider/
Thomas and Matsutani on Hanako-san; Best/Horiuchi on Halloween candy
tampering). As with the existing `sources.md` Phase 3 pass, I did not
independently fetch and read full primary academic texts (Langlois 1978,
Dundes, Best & Horiuchi 1985) — descriptions above are built from
search-tool summaries of those sources and their citing secondary
literature, reliable at the level of a well-sourced summary, not a
verbatim citation. Reddit was not consulted for this pass (prior passes
already logged that direct fetch of reddit.com fails in this environment)
and no candidate here depended on it.
