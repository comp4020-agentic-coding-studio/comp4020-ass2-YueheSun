# Theory audit — what's real, simplified, or fabricated

Scope: every place the site presents a theory, tradition, or framework as
established/circulating, checked against real sources found by web search
on 2026-09-21. Exact current wording quoted from `src/content/sessions/`
and `src/content/lectures/` as of this date.

---

## 1. "Cosmic prisoner theory"

**Where it appears:** `sessions/10-competing-frameworks.md` — "anomalous
sites are containment failures around something already contained
elsewhere, leaking rather than originating locally."

**Search result:** No hits for the exact phrase "cosmic prisoner theory" in
connection with SCP Foundation, creepypasta, or paranormal research
communities. Searches surface only generic SCP background (the Foundation
as a fictional body containing cosmic-horror entities, originating from a
2007 4chan /x/ post — see [SCP Foundation, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SCP_Foundation)),
not this specific named framework. SCP fan discourse does have a real,
loosely analogous concept — GOI (Groups of Interest) "containment failure"
narratives are a recognized SCP structural trope — but "cosmic prisoner
theory" as a phrase or named theory does not appear anywhere searchable.

**Verdict: FABRICATED.** No traceable real source, and no evidence it's
even circulating fan vocabulary — it reads as an invented name styled after
SCP-adjacent "theory of everything" fan lore, but isn't actually that lore.

## 2. "Shadow domain theory"

**Where it appears:** `sessions/10-competing-frameworks.md` — "anomalous
behaviour is bleed-through from an adjacent, structurally similar domain."

**Search result:** No hits for "shadow domain theory" as a named framework
in SCP, creepypasta, or paranormal contexts. The closest real material: a
fan-run battle/verse wiki's power-cataloguing glossary defines "Shadow" as
"the conceptual projection of an object or event into an adjacent
universe," used to compare fictional characters' abilities across
franchises — not part of SCP canon, not a "theory," and not really about
domains ([Alt Battles Wiki](https://alt-battles.fandom.com/wiki/SCP_Foundation)).
Separately, "shadow government" is sometimes used loosely to describe the
SCP Foundation's own opaque leadership structure (the O5 Council) — an
unrelated usage of "shadow."

**Verdict: FABRICATED.** No real term of art under this name in any fan or
scholarly community found. Same styling pattern as item 1 — a plausible-
sounding invented "-domain theory" name, not an actual circulating idea.

## 3. The "fourth-dimensional hypothesis"

**Where it appears:** `sessions/11-fourth-dimensional-hypothesis.md` — "a
structurally-adjacent higher-dimensional space intersects ordinary space at
specific, unstable points."

**Search result:** The *general idea* — hidden/higher dimensions
intersecting ordinary reality, accessed at specific unstable points or
through altered perception — is real and has real currency, but it's older
and broader than SCP/Backrooms fandom. Religious-studies scholar Jeffrey J.
Kripal is quoted describing exactly this lineage: "this interdimensional
reading, long a staple of Spiritualism through the famous 'fourth
dimension,' would have a very long life within ufology and is still very
much with us today." The Backrooms mythos (a real, well-documented
creepypasta franchise, originating from a 2018–2019 4chan thread) is
explicitly extradimensional and organized around "noclipping" through
unstable points into other levels — see
[The Backrooms, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Backrooms) and
[Interdimensional UFO hypothesis, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interdimensional_UFO_hypothesis).
So: the *shape* of the department's hypothesis (thresholds/counts/timing as
markers of dimensional instability) is a real, recognizable trope family —
but "the fourth-dimensional hypothesis" is not itself a named theory in any
of these communities; it's a description the course made up in the style of
one.

**Verdict: REAL-BUT-SIMPLIFIED / FABRICATED NAME.** The underlying trope
(unstable dimensional intersection points, spiritualist "fourth dimension"
lineage feeding into ufology and into Backrooms/SCP-style horror) is real
and traceable. The specific named "fourth-dimensional hypothesis" as a
labelled theory is not found anywhere — it's an original construction built
from a real trope family, not a citation of one. If the course wants this
to be *recognizable* as belonging to a real subgenre rather than presented
as if it were literature the department found, it should signal the
lineage explicitly (Spiritualism's "fourth dimension" → ufology →
Backrooms/SCP extradimensional tropes) rather than naming it as though it
were a specific circulating theory with its own literature.

## 4. "Feng shui boundary theory" and "fú talismans"

**Where it appears:** `sessions/03-comparative-warding.md` — "orientation
and flow across a space, talismans placed at points the theory predicts are
structurally weak."

**Search result:** Fú (符) talismans are real. They are a genuine, well-
documented category of Taoist ritual object: paper charms naming a deity,
stating a command, and closing with an enforcement formula (most commonly
敕令, "by decree"), traditionally brush-written in cinnabar ink on yellow
paper by an ordained Taoist priest, and requiring consecration to be
considered efficacious rather than decorative (see
[Taoism & Manifest](https://www.taoismandmanifest.com/blogs/all/fu-talisman-taoist-paper-charm-home),
[the-taoism-for-modern-world.com](https://www.the-taoism-for-modern-world.com/taoist-protection-talismans/)).
Door/threshold placement is real too — Feng Shui treats the front door as
"the mouth of Qi," and traditional courtyard houses use spirit walls (影壁)
opposite the main gate specifically because "ghosts can only travel in
straight lines" and cannot turn — a genuine, specific, and unusually
concrete piece of real folklore that the course currently doesn't use at
all. The phrase **"feng shui boundary theory," however, is not a real named
doctrine** — feng shui is a real discipline about qi flow, orientation and
placement, but it isn't organized around a single named "boundary theory";
that label appears to be the course's own compression/invention of real
underlying material.

**Verdict: REAL-BUT-SIMPLIFIED.** Fú talismans, priest consecration,
door/threshold placement, and the "ghosts travel only in straight lines"
spirit-wall belief are all real and independently citable. The specific
umbrella name "feng shui boundary theory" is the course's own coinage
layered on top of real practice, not a term practitioners use. Recommend
either dropping the umbrella name or explicitly presenting it as the
department's own descriptive label for a bundle of real practices, not as
if it were the tradition's own term. The straight-line/spirit-wall detail
is a strong candidate to add — it's concrete, real, and currently unused.

## 5. Western "threshold and boundary practice" (salt lines, iron)

**Where it appears:** `sessions/03-comparative-warding.md` — "salt lines,
iron across an opening, rites performed at the moment of crossing."

**Search result:** Real and well-documented. This is the folklore category
scholars call **apotropaic magic** (protective/repelling ritual, as opposed
to magic that summons or compels). Iron's protective reputation across
British, Irish and broader European tradition (horseshoes, iron nails/keys
in doorframes in Slavic tradition) and salt's use in thresholds, exorcism
rites, and boundary-marking are both independently attested folk-magic
traditions, not a single invented practice — see
[Medium, "Protection Magic Through History"](https://medium.com/@kittieparanormal/protection-magic-through-history-e8128a707f9a)
and [Crooked Key Society](https://crookedkeysociety.com/protective-magic-folklore-iron-salt/).
The doorway-as-vulnerable-transition framing is also real and has a genuine
scholarly anchor: anthropologist Mary Douglas's work on purity/boundary
mechanisms is explicitly cited in this connection by folklore commentary,
and it dovetails directly with Victor Turner's liminality (item 7 below) —
the two traditions the course wants compared (Western threshold rites,
Chinese fú/spirit-wall practice) both plug into the same real academic
liminality literature, which the course currently doesn't cite anywhere
despite naming Turner as a planned Phase 4 source.

**Verdict: REAL.** Iron and salt as apotropaic threshold protections are
genuine, independently-documented European/British/Slavic folk practices.
No fabrication here — if anything, this is the site's most defensible piece
of "theory," and the missing move is citing Douglas/Turner-style liminality
scholarship explicitly rather than treating the convergence with Chinese
practice as a department "discovery."

## 6. Freud, "The Uncanny" (1919)

**Verdict: REAL.** Sigmund Freud, "Das Unheimliche" ("The Uncanny"), 1919.
Core claim: the uncanny is not something wholly alien but "that class of
the frightening which leads back to what is known of old and long
familiar" — the German *heimlich* (homely/familiar) contains its own
opposite (*unheimlich*), so uncanniness is the return of something
repressed and once-familiar rather than the intrusion of the wholly
strange. Central case study: E.T.A. Hoffmann's "The Sandman." See
[Freud Museum London](https://www.freud.org.uk/2019/09/18/the-uncanny/) and
the primary text at [MIT](https://web.mit.edu/allanmc/www/freud1.pdf).

## 7. Mark Fisher, "The Weird and the Eerie"

**Verdict: REAL.** Mark Fisher, *The Weird and the Eerie*, Repeater Books,
London, 2016 (published December 2016, shortly before Fisher's death in
January 2017). Core distinction: the **weird** is a disturbance, "a
sensation of wrongness" produced by something that does not belong; the
**eerie** concerns absence and agency — a failure of presence (something
that should be there isn't) or a failure of absence (something is present
that shouldn't act, or acts without visible agency). Both concern "the
outside," not intrinsically horror. Fisher explicitly critiques Freud's
uncanny as too narrow ("locating the strange within the familiar"). See
[Repeater Books](https://repeaterbooks.com/product/the-weird-and-the-eerie/).

## 8. Victor Turner on liminality

**Verdict: REAL.** Victor Turner, *The Ritual Process: Structure and
Anti-Structure*, Cornell University Press, 1969 — building on Arnold van
Gennep's earlier three-stage rite-of-passage model (separation → transition
→ reintegration). Turner's key concept: **liminality** (from Latin *limen*,
threshold) — the ambiguous, transitional, "betwixt and between" state where
a person has no fixed social status — and its companion concept
**communitas**, the levelling, egalitarian bond formed among people
sharing that liminal state. See summary at
[Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/rite-of-passage/Victor-Turner-and-anti-structure).
This is directly usable for the course's threshold/transition warding
material (item 5 above) — the two are the same concept from two angles.

## 9. Folklore scholarship on urban legends

**Verdict: REAL.** Jan Harold Brunvand, folklorist (PhD, Indiana
University, 1961; professor emeritus, University of Utah), *The Vanishing
Hitchhiker: American Urban Legends and Their Meanings*, W. W. Norton, 1981
— the book that established popular/scholarly study of "urban legends" as
a named genre. Core method: legends aren't just catalogued but tracked
across regional variants to show how a story is reshaped by where and when
it's retold, and what social anxiety each variant encodes (his worked
examples include "The Vanishing Hitchhiker," "The Hook," "The Baby-Sitter
and the Man Upstairs"). See
[Jan Harold Brunvand, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Harold_Brunvand).
Directly usable for Phase 2/3 campus-legend and desk-study material: this
gives the course a real methodological lens ("track variants across
retellings, read the variation as data") to apply to invented SlopU campus
stories, mirroring exactly the department's own comparative-case method.
