# Phase 1 supplement — ordinary-life replacement/supplementary sources

Scope: research-only pass, 2026-09-21, requested after `course-logic-proposal.md`
§0 (the "ordinary-life" design principle) demoted three Phase 1 fiction
sources (Lovecraft's "The Dunwich Horror," Pu Songling's "The Painted
Skin," Lafcadio Hearn's "Hōichi the Earless") from adaptation templates to
literary-comparison-only, because they're exotic-occult/mythological in
register. This file looks for real, verifiable REPLACEMENT and
SUPPLEMENTARY candidates — grounded in something an ordinary student could
plausibly encounter (a household object, a photograph, a game cartridge, a
half-remembered TV show, an old building) — that still teach the three
failure-mode concepts those three demonstrated: a countermeasure with a
detection/neutralisation split, a countermeasure that fails via
object-substitution (wrong-but-similar object used instead of the right
one), and a countermeasure that fails via omission/incompleteness (one
part left uncovered). It also looks for any additional real apotropaic
household practice beyond the five already in `sources.md`, and any other
credible ordinary-object/ordinary-technology horror worth adding.

**14 candidates screened, 13 kept (2 as explicitly borderline/moderate),
1 rejected outright.** Nothing below duplicates material already in
`sources.md` (checked against Phase 1 real traditions, Phase 1 fiction,
Phase 3 corpus, and the Phase 4 literature-synthesis additions) or
`01-theory-audit.md`. No real named private individuals, real deaths, or
identifiable real institutions appear below; historical folk-practice
detail (witch bottles) is described generically, without naming any
specific historical case, for the same reason the existing corpus treats
apotropaic magic generically rather than via a named incident.

### 1. Mezuzah fraud / the "kosher vs. invalid scroll" problem — REPLACEMENT for Painted Skin's object-substitution failure
- **Extends existing item**: `sources.md` Phase 1 item 4 (mezuzah placement)
- **URL**: https://www.kmezuzah.com/article/why-cheap-mezuzahs-may-not-be-kosher ;
  https://www.kmezuzah.com/article/fake-mezuzah-scroll-how-fraud-happens ;
  https://www.kmezuzah.com/article/mezuzah-certification ;
  https://www.kosherklaf.com/post/mezuzah-scroll-error (real-world scribal-error
  example, described generically, no names)
- **Author/site**: Kosher Mezuzah / KosherKlaf (Judaica-certification
  practice sites, citing Shulchan Aruch Yoreh De'ah 285–291 and Rambam,
  Hilchot Mezuzah); standard copyright
- **Language**: English · **Week fit**: 3 (direct extension of the existing
  mezuzah item)
- **Tags**: object-substitution, certification, kosher/pasul, fraud,
  invisible defect

A mezuzah's protective/ritual status depends entirely on the scroll inside
the case, not the case itself — and a scroll only counts if it was
hand-written by a certified scribe on prepared parchment with correct
letter-forms and intent; a printed or photocopied scroll is unconditionally
invalid, and even a single malformed or missing letter voids the whole
scroll. The documented failure mode is exactly an object-substitution
problem: cheap or souvenir mezuzahs sold with a case that looks identical
to a certified one, but containing a printed or improperly-written scroll,
are functionally inert — indistinguishable from the outside, and the buyer
usually has no way to tell without an examiner's inspection. A second,
related failure mode is silent decay: even a genuine scroll must be
professionally re-checked roughly every seven years, because ink can fade
or crack invisibly and render a once-valid scroll invalid without the
household ever finding out. This passes the ordinary-life test cleanly —
it's a real, still-practiced household object, and the failure is banal
paperwork/certification fraud, not monster lore. It teaches almost exactly
what Painted Skin taught (a substituted, lesser object is not equivalent
to the specified one) while adding a second, novel lesson Painted Skin
didn't have: a substitution failure that is invisible from the outside and
only caught by inspection, not confrontation.
**SlopU adaptation**: the department's "Class II boundary reinforcement"
kit (already the in-universe name for item 1's iron/salt material) gets a
companion incident type — a batch of certified wards later found, on
audit, to have been fabricated by an uncertified supplier and issued
anyway, indistinguishable by sight from the real thing. The case file
becomes a lesson in "how do you catch a failure that produces no symptom
until tested," directly reusable for weeks 5–6's classification material.

### 2. English witch bottles — REPLACEMENT for the detection/neutralisation split, partial cover for omission/incomplete-execution
- **URL**: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witch_bottle ;
  https://crookedkeysociety.com/witch-bottle-folklore-counter-curse/ ;
  https://www.academia.edu/690132/Buried_Bottles_Witchcraft_and_Sympathetic_Magic ;
  standard academic citation: Ralph Merrifield, *The Archaeology of Ritual
  and Magic* (London: B. T. Batsford, 1987), pp. 169–174
- **Author/site**: Wikipedia (CC BY-SA); Crooked Key Society (independent
  folklore blog); Ralph Merrifield (Museum of London archaeologist,
  standard academic monograph)
- **Language**: English · **Week fit**: 3
- **Tags**: diagnosis, counter-magic, two-stage procedure, incomplete-seal
  failure, domestic object

Witch bottles are a genuinely two-stage English folk-magic practice, not a
single ritual: first a diagnosis stage (an affliction — illness, ruined
milk, persistent bad luck — is identified by a local cunning man or woman
as supernatural in origin, not a random accident) and only then a separate
construction/neutralisation stage (an ordinary bottle filled with sharp
objects — pins, nails, thorns — plus the afflicted person's hair, nail
clippings, or urine, then sealed and either buried at a property's edge,
hidden in a wall or under a hearth, or heated over a fire). The logic: the
sharp objects intercept and hold the harmful influence, and heating or
burial is what "activates" the countermeasure permanently — as long as the
bottle stays hidden and unbroken. The documented failure case is precise
and mechanical: if the bottle was heated to force it to shatter and it
merely popped its cork instead of exploding, the containment was
considered incomplete and the source of the harm was believed to escape
rather than be destroyed. This is a real, archaeologically attested
practice (bottles are still found sealed in the walls and hearthstones of
old English houses), built from entirely ordinary domestic materials, with
a clean two-function structure (diagnose, then separately neutralise) and
a stated partial-execution failure mode — the closest real-world analogue
found to the Voorish Sign / Powder of Ibn-Ghazi's two-stage structure.
**SlopU adaptation**: the department's real countermeasure kit (already
sketched in `sources.md` item 9's adaptation note as "a detection reagent,
a separate neutralisation protocol") can cite this real two-stage
structure instead of the Lovecraft mechanic — diagnosis first (does the
observed pattern actually match a known case type), then a separate,
distinct containment step — with the witch bottle's "popped cork" standing
in for an in-universe containment unit that fails silently unless a
completion check is run.

### 3. Slender Man mythos (2009, Something Awful) and *Marble Hornets* (2009–2014) — Phase 4 framework-tied case
- **URL**: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slender_Man ;
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marble_Hornets ;
  https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/slender-man ;
  Jeffrey A. Tolbert, "'The Sort of Story That Has You Covering Your
  Mirrors': The Case of Slender Man," *Semiotic Review* (2013) — already
  cited in `sources.md`'s Phase 4 section
- **Author/site**: Eric Knudsen ("Victor Surge," originating creator);
  Marble Hornets creators (Troy Wagner, Joseph DeLage); Wikipedia (CC
  BY-SA); Know Your Meme
- **Language**: English · **Week fit**: 9–10 (Phase 4, directly paired with
  the already-cited Tolbert source), with optional secondary use as a
  Phase 3 global-corpus case
- **Tags**: reverse ostension, collaborative myth-building, ordinary media
  (home video/surveillance footage), forum origin

Confirmed: Slender Man originated on 8–10 June 2009 in a Something Awful
forum "paranormal pictures" photoshop contest, where user Eric Knudsen
("Victor Surge") posted two doctored photographs with invented witness
captions describing child abductions by a tall, faceless figure; other
forum users immediately began adding their own "corroborating" images and
backstories. *Marble Hornets*, a YouTube pseudo-documentary web series
begun ten days later (20 June 2009), presents the figure ("the Operator")
through shaky handheld and static surveillance-camera footage of a
missing student film — an entirely ordinary home-video/CCTV aesthetic, no
occult trapping at all. Confirmed directly: Jeffrey A. Tolbert's
"reverse ostension" scholarship (already cited in `sources.md`'s Phase 4
section) uses Slender Man as its primary, named case study — the paper
argues the mythos inverts the normal legend process, building invented
"experiences" first and only afterward assembling them into a
folklore-styled corpus. This is about as direct a tie as this project will
find between a real, ordinary-media case and an already-verified Phase 4
framework. It passes the ordinary-life test cleanly (a forum thread, a
photoshopped snapshot, a home-video series) and needs no rehabilitation —
unlike the three demoted items, nothing here is occult or mythological in
register.
**SlopU adaptation**: week 9 or 10 pairs a short in-universe "case" built
from invented forum posts and handheld footage with Tolbert's framework
directly, asking students whether the department's own case-file corpus
is closer to documenting real ostension or manufacturing reverse
ostension — reusing the exact question `course-logic-proposal.md` already
flags as sharper than either fabricated week-10 framework.

### 4. *The Stone Tape* (1972, BBC, written by Nigel Kneale) and the "Stone Tape theory" — Phase 4 framework-tied case
- **URL**: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Stone_Tape ;
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stone_Tape_theory ;
  https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/making-sense-of-the-weird-and-the-eerie/
- **Author/site**: Nigel Kneale (writer), dir. Peter Sasdy; Wikipedia (CC
  BY-SA); Los Angeles Review of Books (standard copyright)
- **Language**: English · **Week fit**: 9–11 (Phase 4, direct Fisher tie),
  with optional secondary use in week 1 as an ordinary-building/technology
  hook
- **Tags**: residual haunting, recording medium, ordinary building,
  Fisher's eerie

A team of corporate scientists moves into an old Victorian house to
develop a new recording medium, and discovers that a room in the
basement appears to "play back" the final moments of a past death,
seemingly recorded into the stone fabric of the building itself. This
originated the real, still-circulating paranormal-discourse concept now
called "Stone Tape theory" — the idea that a building's structure can
record and later replay a past traumatic event, used specifically to
explain "residual" hauntings (identical, non-interactive repeats) as
distinct from "intelligent" ones (an apparition that responds to the
living). Confirmed directly: Mark Fisher's *The Weird and the Eerie*
(already cited in `sources.md`'s Phase 4 section) discusses *The Stone
Tape* by name as one of his central worked examples of the eerie — "a
presence where there should be nothing," the building itself as the
haunting's actual agent. The premise is an ordinary old building and an
ordinary piece of recording technology, not an occult object; the
"theory" it spawned is real, named, and still cited (with appropriate
skepticism) in paranormal-research writing today, distinct from the play.
**SlopU adaptation**: pairs directly with Fisher in week 9 or 10 — a
disused campus building (already established site type from Phase 2) gets
an in-universe "recording hypothesis" case file explicitly modeled on
Stone Tape theory, giving Fisher's eerie a concrete worked case the same
week it's introduced, exactly the case-to-concept pairing
`course-logic-proposal.md` §3's Option C recommends folding into how
weeks 9–11 are written.

### 5. "Candle Cove" (2009, Kris Straub) — half-remembered children's television
- **URL**: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candle_Cove ;
  https://www.creepypasta.com/candle-cove/ (original hosted text) ;
  https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/candle-cove
- **Author/site**: Kris Straub (named creator, originally posted to Ichor
  Falls, 15 March 2009); Wikipedia; Creepypasta.com
- **Language**: English · **Week fit**: 1 (theory-framing hook) or Phase 3
  (week 7/8 corpus, non-campus)
- **Tags**: false memory, forum-discussion format, ordinary broadcast
  medium, Freud tie

Told entirely as a forum thread: adult posters gradually "remember" a
disturbing, half-remembered children's TV show called Candle Cove from
their childhood, recalling increasingly inappropriate and violent
episodes and a villain who wants to "grind your skin" — until one poster's
mother reveals that whenever he claimed to be watching Candle Cove as a
child, the television actually showed only static. The horror is entirely
in the gap between a shared, escalating false memory and the flat denial
of a parent who was in the room the whole time. This is an unusually
strong ordinary-life fit: a half-remembered kids' show is something almost
every reader has an analogous real memory of, and the mechanism is
psychological rather than occult — a documented, named, real author, not
anonymous "forbidden" lore. It's also a clean Freud pairing: repressed
childhood material returning as something made retroactively strange is
close to a textbook illustration of "the class of the frightening which
leads back to what is known of old and long familiar."
**SlopU adaptation**: week 1's framing example — an in-universe forum
thread where several students "remember" a specific campus event or
broadcast that official records show never happened, used to open the
course's observation-protocol material with the point that collective
false memory is itself a phenomenon worth logging carefully, before any
warding material is introduced.

### 6. Ben Drowned / "The Haunted Cartridge" (2010, Alex Hall, "Jadusable") — an ordinary secondhand game cartridge
- **URL**: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Drowned ;
  https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/majoras-mask-creepypasta-ben-drowned
- **Author/site**: Alex Hall ("Jadusable," named creator); Wikipedia;
  Know Your Meme
- **Language**: English · **Week fit**: 3 or Phase 3 (week 7/8)
- **Tags**: secondhand object, obvious-fix backfires, digital haunting,
  ARG format

A university student buys a secondhand Nintendo 64 Majora's Mask
cartridge from a stranger and finds an existing save file named "BEN" on
it. Deleting the save file — the obvious, sensible fix — doesn't remove
the problem, it removes the game's ability to refer to the player by name
at all, and the situation escalates from there (glitched graphics, direct
messages appearing to come from inside the game). This is a strong
ordinary-life fit: a used game cartridge bought secondhand is about as
mundane an object as this whole corpus offers, and the "haunting"
propagates through completely ordinary consumer technology (a game
console, a YouTube account, a chat client), not ritual objects. The
teachable concept it offers that nothing else in the current corpus does
cleanly: the standard, common-sense remedy (delete the offending file) is
not neutral — attempting it is itself an escalating action, a useful
counterpoint to countermeasures that fail through *insufficient* action
(Painted Skin, Hōichi) rather than through *taking the wrong corrective
action in the first place*.
**SlopU adaptation**: a department case file about a returned/reissued
piece of equipment (a borrowed recorder, a shared laptop) where the
standard reset/wipe procedure is documented as the specific action that
made a case worse, not better — used to teach that "try turning it off
and on again" is not a safe default action against an anomaly.

### 7. "The Yellow Wallpaper" (1892, Charlotte Perkins Gilman) — an ordinary domestic surface
- **URL**: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1952 (public-domain primary
  text) ; https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Yellow-Wallpaper
- **Author/site**: Charlotte Perkins Gilman (public domain); Project
  Gutenberg; Britannica
- **Language**: English · **Week fit**: 1 (literary-comparison hook,
  parallel role to Hill House)
- **Tags**: domestic object, psychological ambiguity, literary-critical
  scholarship

A woman confined to a room for a "rest cure" becomes fixated on the
room's wallpaper, gradually perceiving a trapped woman moving behind its
pattern, and by the story's end has come to identify with that trapped
figure entirely. First published as a horror/Gothic story in 1892 and
read that way by contemporaries (it drew praise from Lovecraft decades
later as a genuine achievement in isolated-tale horror), it only acquired
its now-dominant feminist-critical reading after Elaine R. Hedges's 1973
afterword revived scholarly interest — meaning it carries a genuine,
well-documented critical literature on top of the primary text, useful if
Phase 4 wants a literary case alongside its theoretical readings. It
passes the ordinary-life test as cleanly as anything in this file: a
patterned wallpaper in a rented room is about as unremarkable a domestic
object as exists, and the horror is produced entirely by the ambiguity of
whether the room is haunted or the narrator's perception is
disintegrating — no monster, no ritual, no occult text.
**SlopU adaptation**: kept as an assigned first-week or Phase 4 reading
alongside Hill House, not as a field-case template — used to widen the
"sometimes there is no procedure, only exposure" comparison set with a
second, independently-sourced example, and to give Phase 4 a literary
case that pairs naturally with Freud's uncanny (a familiar domestic
surface become unreadable) without needing rehabilitation the way the
three demoted items did.

### 9. Witch balls (17th–18th c. England, still made today) — supplementary apotropaic tradition
- **URL**: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witch_ball ;
  https://www.britannica.com/art/witch-ball ;
  https://www.witchballs.com/witch-ball-folklore/
- **Author/site**: Wikipedia (CC BY-SA); Britannica; witchballs.com
  (independent folklore/craft site, standard copyright)
- **Language**: English · **Week fit**: 3 (sixth comparative tradition, if
  wanted)
- **Tags**: passive ward, window placement, trap-and-hold mechanism,
  no documented failure case (flagged)

A witch ball is a hollow decorative glass sphere — genuinely documented
from 18th-century Sussex cottages onward, carried to New England by
colonists and still sold today — traditionally hung in an east-facing
window, on a porch, or from a mantel or rafter. The folk logic is a
trap-and-hold mechanism distinct from every other Phase 1 tradition: a
malevolent spirit or ill-wish is believed to be lured by the ball's
reflective surface, and once it touches the glass it is caught and held
in the web-like strands inside the ball, rather than being repelled
outright the way iron or salt work. This is a genuinely ordinary household
object (a piece of coloured glass decor) with a real, specific placement
tradition, but — flagged honestly — no source found documents a *failure*
case for the witch ball itself the way iron/salt (dissolves, gets tracked
away) or ofuda (needs annual renewal) do; it is included as a sixth real
tradition to widen the comparative set, not as a replacement for any of
the three specific failure-mode concepts.
**SlopU adaptation**: a passive countermeasure category distinct from
active wards — an in-universe "capture-type" ward placed at a window or
opening, contrasted explicitly in the week 3 lecture against the
"repel-type" wards (iron, salt, spirit walls) already in the corpus.

### 10. Nazar / evil-eye amulets (Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, South Asian traditions; ancient origin, ongoing practice) — supplementary apotropaic tradition
- **URL**: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazar_(amulet) ;
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evil_eye
- **Author/site**: Wikipedia (CC BY-SA)
- **Language**: English (secondary sources on a cross-cultural,
  multi-language practice) · **Week fit**: 3 (seventh comparative
  tradition, if wanted — the strongest cross-cultural spread of any item
  in this file)
- **Tags**: self-signalling failure, renewal cycle, ubiquitous ordinary
  object, cross-cultural convergence

The nazar (a blue-and-white glass eye-shaped bead) is one of the most
widely distributed protective objects in ordinary daily life anywhere in
the world — hung inside front doors, worn as jewellery, clipped to car
mirrors, given to newborns — across Turkish, Greek, wider Mediterranean,
Middle Eastern, and South Asian practice. The documented folk mechanism is
distinctive among everything else in this corpus: the amulet is believed
to absorb a directed ill-wish, and it visibly signals when it has done its
job by cracking or shattering — folklore treats a broken nazar not as a
failure but as proof of a *successful* interception, with a standard
two-step customary response (acknowledge what it absorbed, then replace
it) rather than any dread response. This passes the ordinary-life test
about as strongly as anything in this file — it's a mass-produced bead
sold in any market stall — and it teaches a genuinely different
countermeasure concept from anything already in the corpus: a ward that
reports its own status by destroying itself, rather than one that fails
silently (mezuzah) or one whose failure has to be inferred (iron/salt).
**SlopU adaptation**: an in-universe "sacrificial indicator" ward class —
a cheap, replaceable object whose visible damage is the department's only
field-legible signal that a boundary took a hit, giving weeks 5–6's
fieldwork material a countermeasure that a student can actually observe
changing state, rather than only inferring an effect from its absence.

### 11. Mirror-covering during Jewish mourning (shiva / aveilut custom) — supplementary practice, direct Tolbert title tie-in
- **URL**: https://www.chabad.org/library/article_cdo/aid/2969340/jewish/Why-Are-the-Mirrors-Covered-in-a-House-of-Mourning.htm ;
  https://aish.com/covering-mirrors-in-house-of-mourning/ ;
  https://hazlitt.net/feature/behind-draped-mirror
- **Author/site**: Chabad.org; Aish.com; Hazlitt (essay site, standard
  copyright)
- **Language**: English · **Week fit**: 3 (extends the existing mezuzah
  tradition's household register) with an explicit Phase 4 cross-reference
- **Tags**: mirror, threshold-of-death practice, folk/halachic dual
  reading, Tolbert cross-reference

During shiva (the seven-day mourning period), it is customary in many
Jewish households to cover every mirror in the home for the duration of
mourning. Multiple explanations are given side by side rather than one
official doctrine: a practical/behavioural reading (mirrors invite vanity
or self-focus, inappropriate during mourning; one should not pray facing
a mirror), and an older folk-magical reading recorded in Kabbalistic
sources — that a household in mourning is especially vulnerable to
spirits, and that a reflected image can trap or expose a soul to harm at
exactly the moment a death has left a "vacancy." Scholarship notes the
custom likely predates its Jewish adoption, picked up from surrounding
folk practice and only later given rabbinic sanction — an ordinary
household object (a mirror) treated as a genuine point of vulnerability
at a liminal moment (a death in the house), which is directly Turner's
liminality by another name. Worth flagging explicitly: this is the real
custom referenced in the title of Jeffrey A. Tolbert's already-cited
Slender Man paper, "'The Sort of Story That Has You Covering Your
Mirrors'" — a genuine, low-effort bridge between a week 3 tradition and a
Phase 4 citation already in the corpus.
**SlopU adaptation**: a seventh comparative-tradition slide, explicitly
cross-referenced in week 9 or 10 when Tolbert is introduced, so the
paper's title lands as a real practice students already saw in week 3
rather than as an isolated academic flourish.
